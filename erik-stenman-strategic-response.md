## How We Approach an Engagement

We do not begin with a menu of marketing activities. We begin with analysis. Before we recommend anything — any channel, any campaign, any investment — we assess the actual situation of the book and the author:

- **Positioning.** What the book is, who it is genuinely for, and how it sits relative to comparable titles. Not a flattering headline — an honest placement.
- **Audience alignment.** Whether the people who would value the book most can currently find it, and through which discovery paths: search, communities, retailers, recommendations, or direct reputation.
- **Market opportunity and competitive environment.** What is already working in the category, where the gaps are, and what is realistically contestable.
- **Search visibility and digital discoverability.** How the book and the author currently surface — metadata, category placement, keyword relevance, author presence — and where the friction points are.
- **Existing online assets.** What you already own — website, profiles, mailing lists, professional standing — that can be built on rather than rebuilt.
- **Reader pathways.** The sequence by which a stranger becomes a reader and a reader becomes an advocate, and where that sequence currently breaks.
- **Long-term authority.** For an author with a professional reputation, book marketing is inseparable from the standing of the name. We treat them as one system.

Only after that analysis do we define scope, timeline, and cost — in writing, fixed, and tied to named actions and measurable outputs. This is why a single book strategy is never a package. Two books by the same author can require entirely different strategies. Yours, in fact, are a good illustration.

## Your Two Books, as We See Them

**The BEAM Book** is an authority-driven technical resource. Its readers — engineers, developers, architects — do not buy because of advertising. They buy because the book is discoverable at the moment they need it, because the author's name carries weight in the community, and because the work earns citations and recommendations over time. The opportunity here is not a launch spike; it is durable, compounding discoverability. The right focus is reaching the correct technical audience precisely, strengthening the search and metadata infrastructure around the book, and aligning its visibility with your professional reputation — so that the book reinforces the name, and the name reinforces the book. Progress is measured in search presence, referral quality, and sustained engagement from the right readers — not vanity metrics.

Audience development here is not promotion after the fact; it is preparation done early enough to matter. Launch strategy, reader targeting, and pre-release visibility are the levers — and they are sequenced and costed before any of it begins.

I note these distinctions deliberately. If the strategy we proposed for each book looked the same, it would tell you we were selling a template.

## 1. HOMEPAGE

**Function:** Within ten seconds, the page must answer four questions — silently, in order:

| Question in Erik's mind | Required element |

| Why trust you? | Trust indicators above the fold: years operating, engagement count, named methodology, and a visible path to case studies. |

**Required components:**
- **Positioning statement** (as above) — one sentence, followed by a one-paragraph expansion. No slogans.

- **Trust block** — two or three short testimonial excerpts with name, role, and genre attribution (anonymized with role/genre if confidentiality requires; never faceless five-star graphics).

**What to remove (if present):** stock imagery of smiling teams, rotating sliders, growth-hacker vocabulary ("scale," "explode," "10x"), counters of dubious metrics ("1M+ impressions"), popups. Each one is a point against us with this audience.

## 4. CASE STUDIES

**Function:** This is the page that decides Erik. He explicitly asked for examples of previous work, comparable campaigns, and results. Screenshots alone prove access to screenshots. The format below proves thinking.

**Each case study follows a five-part structure:**

1. **Problem** — the situation as it existed: title type, genre, what was failing or missing, why standard approaches had not worked.
2. **Strategy** — what the analysis found and why the chosen direction followed from it. This section is the differentiator; most competitors skip directly to tactics.
3. **Execution** — the specific actions performed, in sequence, with channels named. This answers his "actions performed" and "channels used" questions verbatim.
4. **Measurement** — baseline vs. result across defined metrics over a defined period. Numbers stated conservatively and precisely (e.g., "category ranking moved from ~48,000 to top 1,200 within 11 weeks"), with the confidence to say what *didn't* change.
5. **Learning** — what the engagement revealed, what was adjusted, what would be done differently. A case study that admits a lesson is more credible than a flawless one — flawless reads as fabricated to an analytical reader.

**Portfolio requirements:**
- **Three to five case studies minimum**, selected for *comparability to Erik*: at least one technical/nonfiction title (authority comps for The BEAM Book) and at least one fiction launch (genre-entry comps for The Chain). If exact comps do not yet exist, say so and show the nearest analogs — candor about portfolio gaps outperforms stretching a weak match.
- **Attribution policy stated plainly.** Many clients in this space require confidentiality; name where permitted, describe by genre and profile where not. Stating *why* a client is anonymized is itself trustworthy.
- **References:** a short line inviting direct reference conversations upon serious inquiry — matching the email's commitment, so email and site say the same thing at the same depth.
